The examples are from Aha's home page (`app/(aha)/content.ts`), rewritten on 12 November 2026 after Shubham said the first version read like a junior copywriter's.

## 1. What went wrong the first time

The first version was accurate, plain and built around the offer. It still failed, because it described instead of connecting:

- headings named topics
- the same offer was repeated in the same words in every section
- it spoke about the service ("we") before it spoke to the reader ("you")
- nothing in it could be pictured

None of that is fixed by better adjectives. It is fixed upstream, by knowing exactly who is reading and what is stopping them.

## 2. The brief, before any line

Write this first and keep it at the top of the file that holds the copy, so the next person changes a line knowing why it is there.

```
Who it talks to: one person, described by their situation, not their demographics.
What stops them, in order: the worries in the order they arrive.
The one idea: a single sentence the whole page proves.
How it sounds: who is speaking, to whom, and what words are off limits.
How it is built: which question each section answers.
```

The home page's brief, as written:

- **Who:** one person who has been "managing" for months. They work, they show up, and they have never booked therapy, or tried once.
- **What stops them, in order:** "is mine bad enough?", "who would I be talking to?", "what if I don't know what to say?", "what if I pay and it doesn't work?", "what if someone finds out?".
- **The one idea:** coping is not the same as okay. One free hour with a real therapist, by tomorrow. If it doesn't help, you don't pay.
- **How it sounds:** a clear-eyed friend who happens to know therapists. Their words, not ours. Say "you" and use contractions. No therapy stock phrases.

If you cannot fill in "what stops them", you are not ready to write. Find it in what people actually say: the first lines of intake forms, reviews, support messages, the objections the sales team hears.

## 3. The question ladder

A page is a conversation in which the reader never speaks. Each section answers the next thing they are silently asking, in the order they would ask it. The home page's ladder:

| The reader is thinking            | The section that answers                 |
| --------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| Is this for me?                   | The hero names their state               |
| What's the catch?                 | The offer, in plain terms                |
| Do they get my thing?             | Concerns, in the reader's words          |
| What if it goes wrong?            | The promises                             |
| Who will I talk to?               | Real therapists, in their own words      |
| Did it work for someone like me?  | Stories                                  |
| Which kind of help?               | Counsellor, psychologist or psychiatrist |
| What happens after I click?       | Four steps                               |
| What about the nights in between? | Milo                                     |
| (the remaining small worries)     | Questions, in the order the worries come |
| So?                               | The ask                                  |

To build a ladder for a new page, list the reader's questions first, order them, then decide which section answers each. A section that answers no question is cut. A question with no section is the gap in the page.

## 4. Patterns, with lines from the home page

**Name their state before your offer.** The headline is about the reader, and the offer waits for the line under it.

> You've been coping. That's not the same as okay.
>
> Talk to a real therapist by tomorrow. Your first session is free, and if it doesn't help, you don't pay.

**Use their words, not the category's.** The concern is filed under "Anxiety", but what the reader sees is the sentence they could have written.

> My mind won't switch off.

**Show a moment, not a symptom list.** Three moments from an ordinary week, each one something you can picture.

> Replaying one conversation at 3 am. A tight chest for no reason. Bracing for things that never happen.

**Answer the worry underneath.** The stated worry is the anxiety. The one underneath is "am I being dramatic?".

> that sounds exhausting. and no, you're not being dramatic.

**Give permission.** The hardest part of a text box is the first line, so the line beside it removes the standard.

> i'm milo. say it badly. i'll still get it.

**Handle the real objection out loud.** For therapy it is "is mine bad enough?". It gets its own question, and a direct answer.

> If it has been on your mind long enough to bring you to this page, yes. You don't need a crisis or a diagnosis to talk to someone. People rarely regret coming early. They often regret waiting.

**Make the promise concrete, and say what happens if it fails.**

> The right therapist, or we keep looking. If the first one isn't yours, we match you again, free, as many times as it takes. No awkward conversation.

**Use rhythm for the line that must land.** Short, parallel, then stop.

> Not your manager. Not your college. Not your family.

**Turn a feature into a contrast the reader already feels.**

> Therapy is one hour. Milo is there for the other 167.

**Tell them what the first step costs them.** It lowers the step.

> Four steps. The first is the hard one.

**End by calling back to the opening.** The page opens on "okay" and closes on it.

> Okay starts with one free hour.

**Vary how the offer is said.** The offer appears many times on a page, and each time it should do a different job: the hero states it, the promise explains it, the steps place it in time, the close turns it into the ask. Repeating the same sentence four times reads as a template.

## 5. Headings

A heading is the one line most readers will read in a section. Make it a claim or a question the reader would nod at, never a label for what follows.

| A label                    | A heading                                 |
| -------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| Our therapists             | Meet them before you book                 |
| Concerns we help with      | Which one sounds like you?                |
| Types of care              | Counsellor, psychologist or psychiatrist? |
| Our guarantee              | Whatever it takes. In writing.            |
| How it works               | Four steps. The first is the hard one.    |
| Frequently asked questions | What people ask before they book          |
| Testimonials               | People who were where you are             |

The test: could this heading sit on a competitor's page unchanged? If yes, it is a label.

The small label above the heading can stay a label. That is its job. See the next section.

## 6. Small words

The voice lives in the small words as much as the large ones.

- **Section labels** are short and plain, and where possible in the reader's terms: "Start anywhere", "Our word", "Who you'll talk to", "Between sessions". On a buyer's page they can be plainer still: "The problem", "What you get", "How it works", "Questions".
- **Buttons say what the reader gets**, in a few words: "Book my free session", "Try it free". Not "Submit", "Learn more" or "Get started" on its own. A header button can use the short form.
- **The line under a button removes the last hesitation**: "No credit card required", "No spam. No obligations."
- **Field labels are the old hints**, set above the field in the same words, so nothing is lost when a placeholder disappears.
- **Empty and error states are sentences**: "No articles found in this category."
- **Milo speaks in lower case, in the handwritten face**, and never more than a line or two.

Words to keep out: journey, healing, safe space, you are not alone, take the first step, empower, holistic, seamless, solution, and exclamation marks.

## 7. Proof and honesty

Persuasion here never comes from saying something untrue.

- **Numbers come with a source.** Either Shubham supplied them or a page already publishes them. Put a comment beside each one saying which, and when. If you could not check a figure, say that in the comment and in the report.
- **Figures must not contradict each other.** If a headline gives one count and the list beneath it shows another, raise it. Do not quietly pick one.
- **No invented reviews.** A story written for a concept carries a visible "Sample story" tag and a footnote. Real, consented words replace it when they exist.
- **No new promises.** The promises on a page are the ones signed off. Do not add a seventh because it would sound good.
- **Credentials as stated.** Use a person's qualifications as they give them. Ask before a name and photo go public.
- **Crisis language stays plain and complete**: who to call, the number, that Aha is not an emergency service.
- **Overstatement is a decision for the owner.** If a concept page is meant to promise more than the product does today, write the promises as asked and mark them in the copy file as concept promises, so nobody ships them by accident.

## 8. The editing pass

Read the page once as the reader, top to bottom, out loud if you can. Then check:

- Does the first screen name the reader's state?
- Is every heading a claim or a question?
- Does each section answer the next question, in order?
- Is there at least one moment you can picture?
- Is the main objection said out loud and answered?
- Is the offer said differently each time it appears?
- Does the close call back to the opening?
- Any sentence that starts with "we" that could start with "you"?
- Any adjective standing in for a fact? Replace it with the fact or cut it.
- Any stock phrase? Any exclamation mark?
- Any em dash or en dash? Replace with a comma, a colon, a hyphen or a full stop.
- Does every number have its source noted?

Cut a third. The second draft of a line is almost always shorter than the first.

## 9. What to hand back

- The copy in place, in the file that holds it, with the brief at the top.
- For the headline and the main button, two or three alternatives, each with the reason it might win.
- A short list of what in the copy is not yet true in the product, or not yet checked.
- If the request was design only, no copy changes at all, and a note of any line you would change if asked.
